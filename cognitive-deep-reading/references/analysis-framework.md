# Analysis Framework

Use this reference when the source is substantial, controversial, technical, research-heavy, or when the user explicitly wants deeper cognitive gains.

## 1. Argument reconstruction

Capture five elements:

1. **Question** — what problem is actually being answered?
2. **Claim** — what conclusion is asserted?
3. **Evidence** — observations, data, examples, authorities, or cases used.
4. **Mechanism** — why should the evidence imply the claim?
5. **Scope** — where/when/for whom does the claim apply?

A polished essay often hides gaps between evidence and mechanism. Reconstruct those gaps explicitly.

## 2. First-principles reconstruction

Do not ask merely “what assumption is the author making?” Rebuild the problem independently:

- Who are the actors?
- What are their objectives and incentives?
- What scarce resources or constraints matter?
- What state variables determine outcomes?
- What is the baseline or counterfactual?
- What causal mechanism turns inputs into outcomes?
- What time scale matters?
- Which parts are definition-level facts versus empirical regularities?

Turn the answers into a short independent model: starting conditions -> mechanism -> expected outcome. Then compare that model with the author's. Name the premise that must change for their conclusions to diverge. This prevents fashionable theories from being treated as axioms.

Do not mistake familiar beliefs for fundamentals. A first-principles premise should be a definition, a directly checkable constraint, or an empirical regularity whose uncertainty is made explicit. If the problem is normative, state the objective and tradeoffs before deriving a recommendation.

## 3. Load-bearing assumptions

Rank assumptions by sensitivity, not by count. A useful test is:

> If this assumption changes within a plausible range, does the conclusion materially change?

If yes, it is load-bearing and deserves evidence. If no, mention it only if needed for completeness.

## 4. Causal checks

When a source claims A causes B, consider:

- reverse causality: B -> A;
- common cause: C -> A and C -> B;
- selection effects: observed cases differ systematically;
- measurement: proxy differs from the intended concept;
- survivorship: failures are missing;
- intervention effects: the rule changes behavior once people react to it;
- time lag: short-run and long-run effects differ;
- heterogeneity: effects differ across populations or regimes.

Do not mechanically list every issue. Use only those that could plausibly change the conclusion.

## 4a. Critical-reading audit

For each conclusion that matters, trace `claim -> cited evidence -> inferential bridge -> scope`. Find the weakest link that could change the takeaway. In particular:

- Is the claim descriptive, causal, predictive, or normative? A true description alone does not prove a cause or prescription.
- Is the evidence representative and measured in a way that matches the claim?
- Is the comparison against a realistic alternative, with costs and opportunity costs included where relevant?
- Does the author's wording express possibility, average tendency, or necessity? Does the evidence reach that strength?
- What plausible observation would count against the claim? If none is imaginable, explain that the claim is hard to test rather than pretending to falsify it.

Give a proportionate verdict: supported, conditionally supported, plausible but under-evidenced, or contradicted by specified evidence. An absent citation warrants uncertainty; it does not by itself prove the opposite.

## 5. Competing explanation test

A useful alternative must explain the same phenomenon through a different mechanism. For each serious alternative ask:

- What variable does it add or reinterpret?
- What does it predict that the author's model does not?
- What observation would discriminate between them?
- Which explanation has stronger evidence and over what domain?

Avoid cosmetic “economic / psychological / sociological” labels without mechanisms.

## 6. Cognitive-update patterns

Prefer updates such as:

### Distinction
A single vague concept separates into two mechanisms that behave differently.

### Boundary
A universal rule becomes conditional on scale, regime, population, or time horizon.

### Missing variable
A previously ignored constraint or incentive explains apparent contradictions.

### Baseline correction
A recommendation changes after comparing against a real counterfactual rather than an implicit zero baseline.

### Level shift
Two apparently contradictory statements are true at different levels (individual/system, short/long term, local/global).

### Mechanism upgrade
A better causal model explains more observations and makes different predictions.

### Decision-rule update
The analysis changes what one should do when a particular observable condition is present.

## 7. Quality checks before answering

Confirm that:

- the summary is not the majority of the answer;
- criticism addresses the strongest source-supported argument, not a straw man;
- missing discussion is not described as intentional omission without evidence;
- lack of evidence is not called falsity;
- external positions are sourced rather than invented;
- later evidence is not used to unfairly judge what the author could have known at publication time;
- “first principles” are not decorative slogans;
- the analysis independently derives the central mechanism rather than only naming the author's assumptions;
- criticism identifies the inferential step and its effect on the conclusion, rather than listing generic biases;
- at least one section delivers a concrete cognitive update rather than a reformulation;
- uncertainty remains visible where evidence cannot decide.
